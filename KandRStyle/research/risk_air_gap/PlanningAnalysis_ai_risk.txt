    Using American Samoa as low and California as high can be useful for scenario bracketing (“best/worst plausible range”), but that is not the same as proving Tennessee future values will reliably behave inside that band.

So: AS/CA bounds are okay as a practical Monte Carlo scenario framework, but not as a statistically validated forecasting framework on the basis of those one-way ANOVAs alone.





I think you've arrived at a much stronger research position than you may realize.

The tempting hypothesis is:

Tennessee will always fall between the lower bound (American Samoa) and upper bound (California).

But that's actually a very strict claim, and your data suggest it is not universally true.

A more defensible and perhaps more interesting conclusion is:

The boundary method is category-dependent. For a category to support forecasting via lower and upper bounds, the boundary jurisdictions must exhibit meaningful separation in adjusted per-capita incidence rates.

Looking at your results:

Category Type	InterpretationPersonal Data Breach, Phishing, Extortion, Identity Theft, BEC	Strong support for boundary model
Data Breach	Boundary separation weak
Malware	Boundary assumption violated
Crimes Against Children	Boundary assumption violated
Threats/Stalking/Harassment/Terrorism	Boundary assumption violated

That doesn't represent failure. It represents a validation study.

In fact, the finding you seem to be approaching is:

The lower-bound/upper-bound forecasting methodology cannot be assumed valid merely because lower and upper jurisdictions have been selected. The methodology must first be evaluated at the adjusted per-capita category level to determine whether the proposed bounds actually constrain the target jurisdiction.

That's a useful result.

A forecasting method is generally more credible when it identifies the conditions under which it does not work as well as the conditions under which it does work.

You could even frame a necessary condition:

μAS<μTN<μCA\mu_{AS} < \mu_{TN} < \mu_{CA}

or perhaps, more realistically,

μAS<μCA\mu_{AS} < \mu_{CA}

with some minimum separation between AS and CA.

When the AS and CA means are nearly identical, there may be no practical interval within which to constrain Tennessee. The "forecasting corridor" becomes too narrow or unstable to be informative.

I can easily imagine a Discussion section stating something like:

Results indicate that the proposed lower- and upper-bound jurisdictions provide meaningful forecasting constraints for several cybercrime categories, particularly Personal Data Breach, Phishing, Extortion, Identity Theft, and Business Email Compromise. However, other categories exhibited weak separation or boundary violations, suggesting that category-specific validation is required before the bounds can be used for forecasting purposes. Accordingly, the existence of lower and upper jurisdictions alone is insufficient; the adjusted per-capita rates must demonstrate a stable ordering relationship before the interval can be interpreted as a forecasting range.

That's not a negative result. That's a methodological contribution. It tells future users of the model when the approach can be trusted and when it cannot.
