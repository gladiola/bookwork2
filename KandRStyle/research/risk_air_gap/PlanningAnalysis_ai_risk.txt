What you're trying to show is a little different from a traditional hypothesis test. Statistically, you cannot really prove that Tennessee "should" fall between the American Samoa and California bounds. Instead, you can test whether the Tennessee values are consistent with being inside the interval defined by those jurisdictions.

The best test depends on exactly what data you have.

Scenario 1: You have 7 annual observations for each jurisdiction

For each cybercrime category:

American Samoa: 7 yearly values
Tennessee: 7 yearly values
California: 7 yearly values

You could:

A. Confidence Interval Approach (Recommended)
Estimate the mean and confidence interval for Tennessee.
Estimate the mean and confidence intervals for American Samoa and California.
Check whether the Tennessee mean and its confidence interval lie between the estimated lower and upper bounds.

This is simple and easy to explain.

B. Equivalence / Interval Testing

Define hypotheses:

H₀: μTN ≤ μAS or μTN ≥ μCA
H₁: μAS < μTN < μCA

This is essentially an intersection-union test.

Conduct two one-sided tests:

Test whether Tennessee mean is greater than American Samoa mean.
Test whether Tennessee mean is less than California mean.

If both are significant, you have evidence that Tennessee lies between the two.

Scenario 2: You have category means only

If the 7-year means are all you possess (no annual observations), then formal hypothesis testing is difficult because you cannot estimate variability.

You would need:

yearly observations,
standard deviations, or
standard errors

for each jurisdiction.

Without variability measures, you can only show descriptively that:

xˉAS<xˉTN<xˉCA\bar{x}_{AS} < \bar{x}_{TN} < \bar{x}_{CA}

but not determine whether the differences are statistically meaningful.

Scenario 3: Multiple Cybercrime Categories

If there are many cybercrime categories, a stronger analysis is:

Rank Correlation

Check whether Tennessee consistently falls between American Samoa and California across categories.

Mixed-Effects or Repeated-Measures Model

Treat:

Year as a repeated factor,
Crime type as a factor,
State as a factor.

Then test whether Tennessee's expected value is significantly bounded by the other two states.

With only seven years, however, the sample size is small, so simpler methods are often preferred.

For a publication or thesis, I would perform:

Compute annual means for each state and crime category.
Conduct two one-sided t-tests:

Tennessee < California
Report 95% confidence intervals for all means.
Show graphically that Tennessee lies within the interval formed by American Samoa and California.

This directly addresses the claim that Tennessee's cybercrime rate lies between the established lower and upper bounds while providing inferential evidence rather than relying solely on descriptive statistics.

One Concern

ANOVA assumes approximately normal residuals and equal variances.

Cybercrime counts are often:

skewed,
highly variable,
sometimes contain zeros.

If these are raw incident counts, a thesis committee may prefer:

log-transformed counts, or
a generalized linear model (Poisson or negative binomial).

If your observations are annual rates or already averaged over populations, traditional ANOVA is usually easier to justify.

My suspicion is that with only 7 years × 11 categories, a repeated-measures ANOVA with planned contrasts would be understandable to readers and sufficient for a master's thesis, while a mixed-effects model would be the stronger choice if you intend to publish the results.
